Sight-In Targets for 50 Yards — Rimfire Zeroes and the 50/200 Idea

Fifty yards is the most versatile line at the range: far enough that small angular errors show, close enough that conditions barely matter. It's the classic rimfire zero, the standard starting point for carbines, and a gentler proxy for 100 than the 25-yard line. Print the right target and it's also one of the fastest sight-ins there is.

What a 50-yard zero buys you

Rimfire: this is the zero

For a .22 doing plinking or small-game duty, 50 yards is the standard final zero — close to the far crossing of most rimfire trajectories and the distance most rimfire targets get shot at. Zero here, know your holds inside and beyond, done.

Carbines: the 50/200 logic

With many common carbine loads, a bullet zeroed at 50 climbs only modestly above the line of sight and comes back through it far downrange — which is why "50/200" is shorthand at every range. The result is a flat practical hold over the distances a carbine actually gets used. The exact far crossing rides on your load and sight height; the app's solver shows your actual arc rather than the folklore version. Related reading: the 36-yard zero, its main rival for carbines.

Precision and hunting rifles: a proxy, not the finish

A 50-yard group is a fine rough-in for a rifle that will live at 100 and beyond — offsets are half the size they'd be at 25, so errors multiply less. Rough-in at 50, finish and confirm at 100.

The three-shot sight-in workflow

  1. Print the target at 100% scale ("actual size" — never "fit to page"). Every sheet has a 1-inch calibration bar so you can check your printer with a ruler.
  2. Shoot a 3-round group from a solid rest, aiming center.
  3. Photograph the target with the Zero Considerations app. The coded corner markers set true scale and correct camera tilt, the bullet holes are found automatically, and the app converts your group's offset into the exact click count for your optic's click value.
  4. Dial the correction, then shoot a confirmation group. A group is a sample — the confirmation group is what makes it a zero.
