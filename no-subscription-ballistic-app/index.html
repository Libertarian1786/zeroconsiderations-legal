Ballistic calculator apps without a subscription (2026)

If you shoot enough to want a solver in your pocket, you've watched the market shift under you. Apps that used to cost a few dollars once now want a monthly fee, the biggest name in the category sells tiers, and the old default answer — Strelok — can't be downloaded at all. This page maps the honest landscape: which serious apps still sell one-time, which went subscription and why, and which are free because an ammo maker pays for them.

Shooter — $19.99 once

One of the oldest paid solvers still on sale, and its developer has kept the promise most others broke: one-time purchase, free updates. It's a straight ballistic calculator — rifle and load profiles, atmospherics, trajectory tables in MOA and MIL. No photo tools, no zeroing workflow. Note the purchase is per platform: buying on iOS doesn't cover Android.

GeoBallistics — free, $14.99 unlocks Pro

Free to run with one rifle; a one-time $14.99 Pro unlock adds unlimited rifles, range-card save and export, Kestrel connectivity, and online backup. Its distinctive feature is mapping — plotting shots over real terrain — which makes it popular with hunters planning positions. Vortex has promoted it, which tells you the solver is taken seriously.

Ballistic-X — $7.99 base

The best-known group-measurement app: photograph your target, mark your point of aim and each impact, and it computes group size and a scope correction. Since v3.0.3 (June 2025) it ships its own printable BX targets that set the photo's scale automatically. The base app is a one-time $7.99; be aware its optional Advanced Toolkit is sold as a yearly in-app purchase, so read the listing if you want every feature. It measures groups — it isn't a full trajectory solver.

The subscription camp

Applied Ballistics Quantum is the serious long-range platform, and it's tiered: a free Ultralite level, with the full Elite feature set available as a subscription — about $2.99 a month or $19.99 a year as of this writing — or unlocked free when the app pairs with AB-enabled hardware like a compatible Kestrel. To be fair about it: what the subscription funds is real. Custom Doppler-measured drag models for specific bullets, aerodynamic jump and spin drift modeling, profile sync across devices — that data cost real money to shoot and maintain. If you run ELR matches with a Kestrel on your belt, it can be worth every cent.

The objection isn't the price, which is modest. It's the model: your solver access stops the day you stop paying, and a rifle you zeroed under last year's subscription doesn't care that this year's lapsed.

Free, because the ammo maker is paying

Hornady 4DOF is genuinely free with no catch. It's also technically interesting: for bullets in its library it solves from measured drag coefficients rather than a G1/G7 BC, and the library covers Hornady plus selected match bullets from Sierra, Berger, Lapua, and Nosler. The limits are structural, not hidden — the library centers on supported bullets, and it's a trajectory calculator, not a zeroing workflow.

Lapua Ballistics is free and runs a 6DOF model with Doppler-verified data for Lapua's own bullets — excellent if that's what you shoot; anything else is manual entry. Federal offers a free calculator built around its factory loads too.

The pattern is the same everywhere: these apps exist to sell ammunition. Nothing wrong with that — free is free, and Hornady's math is legitimately good — but development follows the brand's priorities, and the feature set stops at drop tables. None of them will measure your group from a photo or tell you how many clicks to turn.

Why subscriptions took over

Three reasons, in descending order of legitimacy:

  • Some features really do cost ongoing money. Doppler radar drag libraries, cloud sync, licensed data, weather services. If an app keeps servers running for you, someone has to pay for the servers.
  • App-store economics. One-time revenue decays as the buyer pool saturates, while maintenance never stops — every OS release breaks something. A subscription smooths that out for the developer.
  • Because enough users accepted it. Plenty of apps charge monthly for features that run entirely on your phone. That's reason three wearing reason one's jacket.

Why a zeroing tool doesn't need one

Here's the thing the subscription pitch glosses over: the solver math is local. Every consumer ballistic app runs a point-mass (or modified point-mass) integration a modern phone finishes in milliseconds — this math ran fine on 2010 hardware. The standard drag models are public. Reticle subtensions come from manufacturers' published spec sheets. Measuring a shot group from a photo is on-device computer vision. Nothing in the zeroing job requires a server, an account, or a signal.

And at the range, you frequently don't have a signal. Rifle ranges sit in valleys, behind berms, past the edge of coverage — exactly where the app has to work. An app that phones home to unlock features is making an architecture choice, not meeting a necessity. For a tool whose whole job happens at a concrete bench in a dead zone, offline isn't a feature. It's the spec.

Is there a catch with the free corporate apps?

No fee, no tricks. The trade-off is structural: bullet libraries built around the maker's products, and nothing beyond trajectory solutions — no photo measurement, no click workflow.

Do these apps need internet?

The math never does. Some apps need a connection for accounts or sync; the good offline ones work in a dead zone and treat weather download as optional.
