Zeroing a Pistol Red Dot: 10, 15, or 25 Yards?

Slide-mounted dots have taken over, and so has the argument about what distance to zero them. The honest answer: with common 9mm loads it matters less than the internet suggests — and the things that actually ruin pistol-dot zeros are click math, shooter wobble, and hardware settling, not the distance you picked.

Picking the distance

  • 10 yards — the easiest distance to shoot a clean group, which makes it the easiest distance to get a true zero instead of a wobble-shaped one. A very good default, especially for a first dot.
  • 15 yards — the compromise pick. Still shootable, a little more information per group.
  • 25 yards — the most demanding and the most informative. Groups open up from shooter input, but a confirmed 25-yard zero tells you the most about the gun.

Here's why the stakes are low: 9mm is flat at pistol distances. For common loads, the difference in point of impact between a 10-yard and a 25-yard zero is on the order of an inch or less anywhere inside 25 yards. Pick one, shoot it well, and confirm at the others. A popular workflow — and the one we'd suggest — is zero at 10, confirm at 25.

Height over bore at close range

A slide-mounted dot sits roughly 0.75 to 1 inch above the bore — much less than a carbine's 2.5 inches (that's why the 36-yard-zero geometry doesn't transfer to pistols), but not nothing. Inside a few yards, the bullet strikes about that far below the dot no matter where you zeroed. On a chest-sized target it's irrelevant; on a small, precise target up close it's the difference between the shot you called and the shot you got. Know the offset; don't fight it.

The click math that catches everyone

Most pistol dots adjust in 1 MOA clicks — about 1 inch per click at 100 yards. At 10 yards, that same click moves impact only about a tenth of an inch. Printing an inch left at 10 yards means something like ten clicks of correction, and this is where most botched dot zeros come from: people dial 2–3 clicks, see nothing move, and start doubting the optic.

Slide-mounted quirks worth respecting

  • The optic rides the slide. Your dot accelerates violently with every shot. Quality mounts hold, but screws settle — re-confirm your zero after the first few hundred rounds on a new install, and put witness marks on the screws.
  • Zero with your carry load. Different bullet weights often print to visibly different heights from a pistol. A zero shot with cheap range ammo is a hypothesis about your defensive load, not a fact.
  • Rest the frame, not the slide. Bag the gun so nothing drags on the slide or pushes on the optic while you shoot your zeroing groups.
  • Irons are separate. If you run backup sights, zero the dot first, then regulate the irons on their own — don't chase one with the other.

Common questions

Best zero distance for a carry gun?

Ten or fifteen yards gets you a clean, honest zero; confirm at 25. The trajectory differences are small — shot-to-shot consistency at the bench matters far more than the distance debate.

Why am I hitting low at 3–5 yards?

Height over bore. The bullet starts about an inch below the dot and hasn't climbed to it yet. That offset exists with every zero distance.
